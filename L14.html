Cauchy Sequences in \(\mathbb{R}\)

A complete treatment: definition, convergence equivalence, completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\), examples, counterexamples, and self-assessment.

Formal Definition

A sequence \((x_n)\) of real numbers is called a Cauchy sequence if:

\[\forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\;\exists\,N\in\mathbb{N}:\quad m,n \ge N \;\Longrightarrow\; |x_m - x_n| < \varepsilon.\]

Key point: terms become mutually close — no reference to a limit is needed in the definition.

Intuition
✦ Terms cluster arbitrarily close together as \(n\to\infty\).
✦ No "peeking" at the limit required — purely intrinsic.
✦ In \(\mathbb{R}\): Cauchy \(\Leftrightarrow\) convergent (Completeness).
✦ In \(\mathbb{Q}\): Cauchy does \(\not\Rightarrow\) convergent (gaps!).

Examples and Non-Examples

Constant Sequence Cauchy ✓
x_n = c

\(|x_m - x_n| = 0 < \varepsilon\) for all \(m,n\) and any \(N=1\).

\(x_n = 1/n\) Cauchy ✓
Converges to 0

\(|x_m - x_n| \le \tfrac{1}{m}+\tfrac{1}{n} \le \tfrac{2}{N} < \varepsilon\) once \(N > 2/\varepsilon\).

Alternating \((-1)^n\) Not Cauchy ✗
Diverges

\(|x_{n+1}-x_n| = 2\) for all \(n\). With \(\varepsilon=1\), no \(N\) works.

\(x_n = n\) Not Cauchy ✗
Diverges to ∞

\(|x_m - x_n| = m-n\) can be made arbitrarily large. Take \(\varepsilon=1,\,m=n+2\).

Geometric \(r^n\), \(|r|<1\) Cauchy ✓
Converges to 0

\(|r^m - r^n| \le r^N\cdot\tfrac{1}{1-r}\); choose \(N\) large enough via \(r^N\to 0\).

Harmonic Partial Sums Not Cauchy ✗
\(H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k\)

\(H_{2n}-H_n > \tfrac{1}{2}\) for all \(n\). Fails Cauchy with \(\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}\).

Proof: Harmonic Partial Sums are Not Cauchy

Suppose \((H_n)\) were Cauchy. Take \(\varepsilon = \tfrac{1}{2}\). Then for \(n \ge N\) and \(m = 2n\):

\[H_{2n} - H_n = \sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac{1}{k} \;\ge\; \sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac{1}{2n} = \frac{n}{2n} = \frac{1}{2}.\]

This gives \(|H_{2n}-H_n| \ge \tfrac{1}{2} = \varepsilon\) for all \(n\), contradicting the Cauchy property. \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 1: Every Convergent Sequence is Cauchy

This is the easy direction and uses only the triangle inequality.

Proof

Suppose \(x_n \to L\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By convergence, \(\exists\,N\) such that \(n \ge N \Rightarrow |x_n - L| < \varepsilon/2\).

For \(m,n \ge N\):

\[|x_m - x_n| \le |x_m - L| + |L - x_n| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.\]

Hence \((x_n)\) is Cauchy. \(\blacksquare\)

The \(\varepsilon/2\) Trick

We use \(\varepsilon/2\) for each term so the two errors sum to \(\varepsilon\). This is the standard method whenever two approximations are combined by the triangle inequality.

Key Diagram
        L
   ◄─ ε/2 ─►  ◄─ ε/2 ─►
···  x_n        x_m  ···

Both \(x_m\) and \(x_n\) within \(\varepsilon/2\) of \(L\), hence within \(\varepsilon\) of each other.

Lemma: Every Cauchy Sequence is Bounded

This is the first step toward proving that Cauchy sequences converge in \(\mathbb{R}\).

Proof

Let \((x_n)\) be Cauchy. Take \(\varepsilon = 1\). There exists \(N\) such that for all \(m,n \ge N\): \(|x_m - x_n| < 1\).

Fix \(m = N\). Then for all \(n \ge N\): \(|x_n| \le |x_n - x_N| + |x_N| < 1 + |x_N|.\)

Set

\[M = \max\bigl(|x_1|,\,|x_2|,\,\ldots,\,|x_{N-1}|,\;1+|x_N|\bigr).\]

Then \(|x_n| \le M\) for all \(n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Why Boundedness Matters

Knowing \((x_n)\) is bounded allows us to apply the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem:

Bolzano–Weierstrass

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

This gives us a subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) converging to some \(L\in\mathbb{R}\). The next step shows the whole sequence then converges to \(L\).

Theorem 2 (Completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\)): Every Cauchy Sequence Converges

This is the deep theorem. It characterises the completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\). The proof has three steps. (Proof-1: using Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem)

Full Proof

Step 1. By the Lemma, \((x_n)\) is bounded.

Step 2. By the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem, there exists a subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) and \(L\in\mathbb{R}\) with \(x_{n_k}\to L\).

Step 3. We claim \(x_n\to L\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\).

  • Since \((x_n)\) is Cauchy, \(\exists\,N_1\) such that \(m,n\ge N_1 \Rightarrow |x_m - x_n| < \varepsilon/2\).
  • Since \(x_{n_k}\to L\), \(\exists\,N_2\) such that \(k \ge N_2 \Rightarrow |x_{n_k} - L| < \varepsilon/2\).

Choose \(K\ge N_2\) large enough that \(n_K \ge N_1\). For any \(n \ge N_1\):

\[|x_n - L| \le |x_n - x_{n_K}| + |x_{n_K} - L| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.\]

(The first term uses the Cauchy property with \(n, n_K \ge N_1\); the second uses the subsequence convergence with \(K \ge N_2\).) Since \(\varepsilon>0\) was arbitrary, \(x_n\to L\). \(\blacksquare\)

Why \(\mathbb{Q}\) Fails

The sequence of rational decimal approximations to \(\sqrt{2}\): \(1,\,1.4,\,1.41,\,1.414,\ldots\) is Cauchy in \(\mathbb{Q}\) but its limit \(\sqrt{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}\). Completeness fails in \(\mathbb{Q}\).

The Key Equivalence in \(\mathbb{R}\)
\[(x_n) \text{ converges in } \mathbb{R} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; (x_n) \text{ is Cauchy.}\]

\((\Rightarrow)\) Theorem 1   \((\Leftarrow)\) Theorem 2 (Completeness)

Deep Core Theorem

Theorem 2: The Completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\)

Every Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\) converges to a limit inside \(\mathbb{R}.\) We prove this fundamentally via the **Least Upper Bound (LUB) Property** of real numbers.

Proof Configuration: Defining the "Clump" Set

Let \((x_n)\) (or simply x) be a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\). We define an auxiliary collection \(E_x \subset \mathbb{R}\) consisting of all real numbers such that after some stage all terms of the sequence greater than those numbers.

\(E_x = \bigl\{ y \in \mathbb{R} : \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \;\; \forall n\ge N\; (y< x_n) \bigr\}\)

We will leverage the completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\) by showing that \(E_x\) is non-empty and bounded above, meaning its lub \(\alpha = lub(E_x)\) exists and serves as the limit of our sequence.

Part 1Entering the Small Interval

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\) be chosen arbitrarily. Since \((x_n)\) is Cauchy, there is an index anchor \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) such that for all trailing indices \(n, m \ge N\): \[|x_n - x_m| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\]

Let's pin the pointer at \(m = N\). For all remaining elements inside the tail sequence (\(n \ge N\)), the configuration simplifies to: \[|x_n - x_N| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \iff x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} < x_n < x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\]

Geometric Layout: The Real Line Tail Concentration

Part 2E_x is Non-Empty

From our interval clump bounds, we know that for every index coordinate \(n \ge N\), the evaluation yields \(x_n > x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\).

Because this statement applies to the entire infinite tail of the sequence, there are undeniably infinitely many terms greater than \(x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). Consequently, by definition of the set criteria: \[x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \in E_x \;\Longrightarrow\; E_x \neq \emptyset.\]

Part 3 \(E_x\) is Bounded Above

We claim that \(x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) acts as a upper bound for \(S\). Let's prove via contradiction:

Assume there exists an element \(y \in E_x\) such that \(y > x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). Since \(y \in E_x\), there must be infinitely many elements where \(x_n \ge y > x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\).

However, this clearly contradicts our step result stating that all indices satisfying \(n \ge N\) must drop strictly underneath \(x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). Thus, our assumption fails, confirming that: \[\forall y \in E_x, \;\; y \le x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\]

Part 4 & 5lub Trap & Convergence Concluding Phase

Since \(E_x\) is proven non-empty and bounded from above within \(\mathbb{R}\), the **Least Upper Bound Property** dictates that a precise real supremum value exists: \(\alpha = lub(E_x) \in \mathbb{R}\).

Let's bracket this value between our established boundary coordinates:

  • Since \(x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \in E_x\), we must have: \(x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \le \alpha\)
  • Since \(x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) is a certified upper bound for \(E_x\), we must have: \(\alpha \le x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\)

Combining these inequalities yields an error bracket around the anchor value: \[x_N - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \le \alpha \le x_N + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; |x_N - \alpha| \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\]

Finally, we can lock down the limit test. For any tracking tail index index \(n \ge N\), apply the triangle inequality once more:

\[|x_n - \alpha| \le |x_n - x_N| + |x_N - \alpha| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.\]

This proves rigorously that \(\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = \alpha\). Every Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\) converges perfectly. \(\blacksquare\)

When Showing Cauchy is Easier Than Finding the Limit

The Cauchy criterion is most powerful when we don't know the limit. We prove convergence without ever identifying \(L\) — then use algebra to find \(L\) afterwards.

Example A Contraction Mappings — \(x_{n+1} = \tfrac{x_n}{2} + \tfrac{1}{x_n}\)

Define \(x_1 = 2\) and \(x_{n+1} = \tfrac{x_n}{2}+\tfrac{1}{x_n}\).
The limit is \(\sqrt{2}\) — but we don't need to know this to prove convergence!

Key estimate: for \(x_n > 0\),

\[x_{n+1} - x_n = \frac{1}{x_n} - \frac{x_n}{2} = \frac{2 - x_n^2}{2x_n}.\]

One shows \(x_n \ge \sqrt{2}\) for all \(n\ge 2\) (AM–GM), so the sequence decreases and is bounded below — convergent by MCT. Alternatively, the contraction argument below avoids MCT.

Contraction Argument

Let \(f(x)=\tfrac{x}{2}+\tfrac{1}{x}\). Then \(|f'(x)|=|\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{x^2}|\). For \(x \ge \sqrt{2}\): \(|f'(x)| \le \tfrac{1}{2} =: k < 1\).

By the Mean Value Theorem:

\[|x_{n+1}-x_n| \le k|x_n - x_{n-1}| \le \cdots \le k^{n-1}|x_2-x_1|.\]

For \(m > n\ge N\):

\[|x_m - x_n| \le \frac{k^{N-1}}{1-k}|x_2-x_1| \to 0.\]

Cauchy criterion satisfied. \(\blacksquare\)

Example B Series with Decreasing Terms — \(S_n = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\sin k}{k^2}\)

The limit \(S = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\sin k}{k^2}\) has no simple closed form. Yet convergence is easy to prove:

\[|S_m - S_n| = \left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}\frac{\sin k}{k^2}\right| \le \sum_{k=n+1}^m \frac{|\sin k|}{k^2} \le \sum_{k=n+1}^m \frac{1}{k^2}.\]

Since \(\sum 1/k^2\) converges (its partial sums are Cauchy), the right side \(\to 0\). Hence \((S_n)\) is Cauchy, hence convergent. No explicit value of \(S\) needed.

The General Principle

A series \(\sum a_k\) converges \(\Leftrightarrow\) its partial sums are Cauchy:

\[\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^m a_k\right| < \varepsilon \quad\text{for all }m>n\ge N.\]

If \(\sum |a_k|\) converges (absolute convergence), this is immediate: \(|S_m-S_n|\le\sum_{k=n+1}^m|a_k|\to 0\).

Example C General Contractive Sequence

Theorem

If \(|x_{n+1}-x_n|\le k|x_n-x_{n-1}|\) for some fixed \(0 < k < 1\) and all \(n\ge 2\), then \((x_n)\) is Cauchy (hence convergent in \(\mathbb{R}\)).

Proof

Let \(d = |x_2 - x_1|\). Then \(|x_{n+1}-x_n|\le k^{n-1}d\). For \(m > n\):

\[|x_m - x_n| \le \sum_{j=n}^{m-1}|x_{j+1}-x_j| \le d\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}k^{j-1} = \frac{dk^{n-1}}{1-k}\to 0.\]

Hence Cauchy. \(\blacksquare\)

Why this is powerful

The sequence \(x_{n+1}=f(x_n)\) defined by a contraction \(f\) (with \(|f'|<1\)) converges to a fixed point \(f(L)=L\), proved purely by the Cauchy criterion — without knowing \(L\) in advance.

Examples of contractions:
  • \(f(x)=\cos x\) on \([0,1]\) (|f'|=|\sin x|\le\sin 1<1)
  • \(f(x)=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{x}\) for \(x\ge\sqrt{2}\)
  • \(f(x)=1-\frac{x^2}{2}\) near \(x=0\)

When Showing Not-Cauchy is Easier Than Not-Convergent

For divergence, showing the Cauchy property fails is often cleaner than finding two subsequences with different limits. We need: \(\exists\,\varepsilon_0>0\) such that \(\forall N,\;\exists\,m,n\ge N\) with \(|x_m-x_n|\ge\varepsilon_0\).

Not Cauchy \(H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{k}\)

Take \(\varepsilon_0=\tfrac12\), \(m=2n\):

\[H_{2n}-H_n>\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{for all }n.\]

Easier than showing \(H_n\to\infty\) from scratch.

Not Cauchy \(x_n = \sqrt{n}\)

Take \(m=4n\), \(\varepsilon_0=1\):

\[|x_{4n}-x_n|=\sqrt{4n}-\sqrt{n}=\sqrt{n}\ge 1\;\text{for }n\ge 1.\]

Direct from definition; no subsequence argument.

Not Cauchy \(x_n=(-1)^n n\)

Take consecutive \(m=n+1\):

\[|x_{n+1}-x_n|=n+1+(n)=2n+1\to\infty.\]

Immediately not Cauchy; even \(\varepsilon_0=1\) works.

Not Cauchy \(x_n=\ln n\)

Take \(m=en\) (integer part), \(\varepsilon_0=\tfrac12\):

\[|\ln(en)-\ln n|=\ln e = 1 > \tfrac{1}{2}.\]

No need to prove \(\ln n\to\infty\) separately.

Not Cauchy Partial sums \(\sum(-1)^k\)

Consecutive difference is always \(1\):

\[|S_{n+1}-S_n|=|(-1)^{n+1}|=1.\]

Cannot be Cauchy; trivially verified.

The Negation Strategy

To show not Cauchy:

  1. Fix a specific \(\varepsilon_0>0\).
  2. For each \(N\), exhibit \(m,n\ge N\) (often \(m=cn\)) with \(|x_m-x_n|\ge\varepsilon_0\).
  3. This witnesses the failure for every \(N\).

Since Cauchy \(\Leftrightarrow\) convergent in \(\mathbb{R}\), not-Cauchy always implies divergence — but the Cauchy route is often shorter.

The Tail-Sum Criterion for Series

A series \(\sum a_k\) with partial sums \(S_n\) diverges if and only if \((S_n)\) is not Cauchy:

\[\exists\,\varepsilon_0>0:\;\forall N,\;\exists\,m>n\ge N:\; \left|\sum_{k=n+1}^m a_k\right|\ge\varepsilon_0.\]

Necessary condition for convergence (contrapositive): If \(\sum a_k\) converges, then \(a_n\to 0\). (Proof: take \(m=n+1\); then \(|a_{n+1}|=|S_{n+1}-S_n|\to 0.\))

Self-Assessment

Part A — Definitions & Basic Proofs

Q1. Write out the definition of a Cauchy sequence and its negation (sequence that is not Cauchy).

Cauchy: \(\forall\varepsilon>0,\;\exists N:\;m,n\ge N\Rightarrow|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon.\)
Not Cauchy: \(\exists\varepsilon_0>0:\;\forall N,\;\exists\,m,n\ge N\text{ with }|x_m-x_n|\ge\varepsilon_0.\)

Q2. Prove directly from the definition that \(x_n = 1/n^2\) is Cauchy.

For \(m>n\ge N\): \(|x_m-x_n|\le\frac{1}{n^2}+\frac{1}{m^2}\le\frac{2}{N^2}.\) Choose \(N>\sqrt{2/\varepsilon}\). Then \(|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon.\;\blacksquare\)

Q3. Show \(x_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt{n}\) is Cauchy.

\(|x_m-x_n|\le|x_m|+|x_n|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\le\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}.\) Choose \(N>(2/\varepsilon)^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Q4. Prove the partial sums of \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{1}{k^2}\) form a Cauchy sequence.

For \(m>n\ge N\): \(|S_m-S_n|=\sum_{k=n+1}^m\frac{1}{k^2}\le\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty\frac{1}{k(k-1)}=\frac{1}{n}.\) (Telescoping: \(\frac{1}{k(k-1)}=\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\).) Choose \(N>1/\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

Q5. Show directly that \(x_n = n^2/(n+1)\) is not Cauchy.

Take \(\varepsilon_0=1\), \(m=n+1\). Then \(|x_{n+1}-x_n|=\frac{(n+1)^2}{n+2}-\frac{n^2}{n+1}=\frac{n^2+2n+1}{n+2}-\frac{n^2}{n+1}\to\infty.\) In particular \(\ge 1\) for large \(n\), so not Cauchy.

Part B — Deeper Results

Q6. If \((x_n)\) and \((y_n)\) are Cauchy, prove \((x_n+y_n)\) is Cauchy.

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N_1,N_2\) for \((x_n),(y_n)\) respectively with \(\varepsilon/2\). For \(m,n\ge\max(N_1,N_2)\): \(|(x_m+y_m)-(x_n+y_n)|\le|x_m-x_n|+|y_m-y_n|<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.\;\blacksquare\)

Q7. Prove: if \((x_n)\) is Cauchy, then every subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) is also Cauchy.

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), let \(N\) work for the Cauchy criterion of \((x_n)\). Since \(n_k\ge k\), for \(k,j\ge N\) we have \(n_k,n_j\ge N\), so \(|x_{n_k}-x_{n_j}|<\varepsilon\). Hence \((x_{n_k})\) is Cauchy. \(\blacksquare\)

Q8. Let \(x_{n+1}=\cos(x_n)\), \(x_1=1\). Show \((x_n)\) is Cauchy without finding the limit.

\(|\cos x - \cos y|\le|x-y||\sin c|\le(\sin 1)|x-y|\) for some \(c\). Set \(k=\sin 1\approx 0.841<1\). Then \(|x_{n+1}-x_n|\le k^{n-1}|x_2-x_1|\). For \(m>n\): \(|x_m-x_n|\le\frac{k^{n-1}}{1-k}|x_2-x_1|\to 0\). Cauchy, hence convergent to the fixed point of cosine (\(\approx 0.739\)). \(\blacksquare\)

Q9. If \((x_n)\) is Cauchy and has a subsequence converging to \(L\), prove \(x_n\to L\).

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(N_1\) so \(m,n\ge N_1\Rightarrow|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon/2\). Choose \(N_2\) so \(k\ge N_2\Rightarrow|x_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon/2\). Pick \(K\ge N_2\) with \(n_K\ge N_1\). For \(n\ge N_1\): \(|x_n-L|\le|x_n-x_{n_K}|+|x_{n_K}-L|<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.\;\blacksquare\)

Q10 (Challenge). Show the partial sums \(T_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\) are Cauchy, hence the alternating harmonic series converges.

For \(m>n\ge N\): \(|T_m-T_n|=\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^m\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\right|\le\frac{1}{n+1}\le\frac{1}{N}.\) (The alternating sum telescopes and is bounded by the first term.) Choose \(N>1/\varepsilon\). Hence Cauchy, hence convergent (to \(\ln 2\)). \(\blacksquare\)

Summary

The Four Key Results
1 Convergent \(\Rightarrow\) Cauchy   (triangle inequality + \(\varepsilon/2\))
2 Cauchy \(\Rightarrow\) Bounded   (take \(\varepsilon=1\), finite max)
3 Cauchy + Bounded \(\Rightarrow\) Convergent subsequence   (Bolzano–Weierstrass)
4 Cauchy + Convergent subsequence \(\Rightarrow\) Full convergence   (\(\varepsilon/2\) again)
