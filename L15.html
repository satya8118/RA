Continuity — Examples, Counterexamples & Proofs

A collection of carefully worked examples illustrating the sequential definition of continuity: functions that are nowhere continuous, continuous everywhere except one point, and continuous everywhere — together with two structural theorems.

Recall — The Sequential Definition of Continuity

Sequential Definition
  1. Let $J$ be a nonempty subset of $\mathbb{R}.$

    $f:J\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $a\in J$ if: $$\text{for every sequence }(x_n)\text{ in }J\text{ with }x_n\to a, \quad f(x_n)\to f(a).$$
  2. Discontinuity at $a$: there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ in $J$ with $x_n\to a$ but $f(x_n)\not\to f(a).$
    What do you mean by $f(x_n)\not\to f(a)?$ either $f(x_n)$ not converge or even if it converges it is not converges to $f(a).$
  • If $f$ is continous at every point in $J,$ then we say $f$ is continous on $J.$
  • This way of defining is very useful, as we use our previous knowledge on sequences.
  • Note that this definition is a local definition: Continous at a point.
  • Finally focus on the domain: $J$ is a nonempty set. It can be a singleton set, entire $\mathbb{R}$ or interval or union of sets etc. For example, if $J=\{0\},$ then constant function $f(x)=0,$ or $f(x)=x, x^2, \sin x , x^3-x,$ etc all are equal.
Two tools we use repeatedly.
  • Density: For any $a\in\mathbb{R},$ there exist sequences of rationals $r_n\to a$ and sequences of irrationals $s_n\to a.$
  • Sign persistence: If $x_n\to a$ and $a>0,$ then eventually $x_n>0.$ If $a<0,$ eventually $x_n<0.$
Strategy for proofs
To prove continuous at $a$: take any sequence $x_n\to a$ and show $f(x_n)\to f(a)$ — often using limit laws or a sandwich argument.

To prove discontinuous at $a$: exhibit a single specific sequence $x_n\to a$ for which $f(x_n)\not\to f(a).$

You may also exhibit two sequences both converging to $a$ but with $f$-values converging to different limits — the uniqueness of limits then gives the contradiction.

Independent of domains

What ever may be the domain, these functions are continuous.

Check the following.

  • Every constant function is continous.
    Let $\forall x\in J,\; \;f(x)=c$ and $a\in J.$
    Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $J$ converges to $a.$
    Then $f(x_1)=f(x_2)=\dots=c$ that is $(f(x_n))$ is the constant sequence $(c).$ Hence $f(x_n)\to c=f(a).$
    Here $a$ is arbitrary, thus $f$ is continous on $J.$
  • Since $J\subseteq \mathbb{R},$ the restriction of identity map $f(x)=x$ to $J$ is continous.
    Let $a\in J.$ Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in J converges to $a.$ Then $f(x_1)=x_1, f(x_2)=x_2,\ldots$ that is $(f(x_n))$ is same as the sequence $(x_n)$ and it converges to $a=f(a).$ Hence $f(x_n)\to f(a).$ Here $a$ is arbitrary, thus $f$ is continous on $J.$
  • Show $f(x)=x^2$ in general $f(x)=x^n$ is continuous.

Example 1 — The Sign Function: Continuous Everywhere Except $0$

The Sign Function
$$\text{sgn}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad \text{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x>0\\ 0 & x=0\\ -1 & x<0. \end{cases}$$ Claim: $\text{sgn}$ is continuous at every $a\ne 0$ and discontinuous at $a=0.$
The sign function has jumps at $0.$ Sequences from the right give images tending to $1;$ from the left to $-1;$ neither equals $f(0)=0.$ For $a\ne 0$ the graph is locally flat — continuous.
Proof — Continuity at $a\ne 0.$ Case $a>0$: Let $(x_n)$ be any sequence with $x_n\to a>0.$ By sign persistence, there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $x_k>0$ for all $k\ge N.$ Hence $\text{sgn}(x_k)=1=\text{sgn}(a)$ for all $k\ge N.$ So $\text{sgn}(x_n)\to 1=\text{sgn}(a).$ $\checkmark$

Case $a<0$: Similarly, eventually $x_k<0,$ so $\text{sgn}(x_k)=-1=\text{sgn}(a)$ for all large $k.$ Hence $\text{sgn}(x_n)\to -1=\text{sgn}(a).$ $\checkmark$ $\blacksquare$
Proof — Discontinuity at $a=0.$ We exhibit three sequences converging to $0$ with incompatible $f$-images:

Let $x_n=\tfrac{1}{n},\; y_n=-\tfrac{1}{n},\; z_n=\tfrac{(-1)^n}{n}.$ All three satisfy $x_n,y_n,z_n\to 0.$
  • $\text{sgn}(x_n)=1$ for all $n,$ so $f(x_n)\to 1\ne 0=f(0).$
  • $\text{sgn}(y_n)=-1$ for all $n,$ so $f(y_n)\to -1\ne 0=f(0).$
  • $(f(z_n))=(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots)$ diverges — certainly not converging to $f(0).$
Each of these alone suffices to prove discontinuity at $0.$ $\blacksquare$
Summary
$\text{sgn}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}=(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)$ and discontinuous at $0.$ It has a jump discontinuity at $0$: the left limit is $-1,$ the right limit is $1,$ and the function value is $0$ — all three differ.

Example 2 — Dirichlet's Function: Nowhere Continuous

Dirichlet's Function
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x\in\mathbb{Q}\\ 0 & x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases}$$ Claim: $f$ is not continuous at any point $a\in\mathbb{R}.$
Dirichlet's function oscillates densely between $0$ and $1$ at every scale — no connected graph is possible. It is discontinuous at every real number.

Proof at $a = 0$ (three illuminating approaches)

Approach 1 — Two sequences giving different limits. Note $f(0) = 1$ (since $0\in\mathbb{Q}$).

Let $x_n = \tfrac{1}{n}\in\mathbb{Q}.$ Then $x_n\to 0$ and $f(x_n)=1$ for all $n,$ so $f(x_n)\to 1 = f(0).$ So far so good.

Let $y_n = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{n}\notin\mathbb{Q}.$ Then $y_n\to 0$ and $f(y_n)=0$ for all $n,$ so $f(y_n)\to 0 \ne 1 = f(0).$

Since there exists a sequence converging to $0$ whose images do not converge to $f(0),$ $f$ is discontinuous at $0.$ $\blacksquare$
Approach 2 — A single sequence whose image diverges. Define $$x_n = \begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{n} & n\text{ odd}\\\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{n} & n\text{ even.}\end{cases}$$ Then $|x_n|\le\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{n}\to 0,$ so $x_n\to 0.$ But $(f(x_n)) = (1,\,0,\,1,\,0,\,\ldots),$ which diverges. Since a divergent sequence certainly does not converge to $f(0)=1,$ $f$ is discontinuous at $0.$ $\blacksquare$
Key insight from Approach 2
Finding a single sequence $x_n\to a$ for which $(f(x_n))$ fails to converge to $f(a)$ — whether because it diverges or converges to the wrong value — is sufficient to prove discontinuity. You do not need the images to converge to two different values.

Proof at a General Point $a\in\mathbb{R}$

Proof. Let $a\in\mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary.

By the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R},$ there exist sequences: $(r_n)$ with $r_n\in\mathbb{Q}$ and $r_n\to a,$ and $(s_n)$ with $s_n\notin\mathbb{Q}$ and $s_n\to a.$

We have $f(r_n)=1$ for all $n$ so $f(r_n)\to 1,$ and $f(s_n)=0$ for all $n$ so $f(s_n)\to 0.$

Case $a\in\mathbb{Q}$: $f(a)=1.$ The sequence $(s_n)$ satisfies $s_n\to a$ but $f(s_n)\to 0\ne 1=f(a).$ Discontinuous.

Case $a\notin\mathbb{Q}$: $f(a)=0.$ The sequence $(r_n)$ satisfies $r_n\to a$ but $f(r_n)\to 1\ne 0=f(a).$ Discontinuous.

In both cases $f$ is discontinuous at $a.$ Since $a$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous nowhere on $\mathbb{R}.$ $\blacksquare$
Continuous only at one point
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad f(x) = \begin{cases} x & x\in\mathbb{Q}\\ 0 & x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases}$$ Check this function is continuous only at $x=0.$

Example — $f(x)=x$ on $\mathbb{Q}$ and $f(x)=1-x$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$

The Function
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f(x)=\begin{cases} x & x\in\mathbb{Q},\\ 1-x & x\notin\mathbb{Q}.\end{cases}$$ Question: At which points $a\in\mathbb{R}$ is $f$ continuous?
Rough Work — Spotting the Key At a point $a,$ two types of sequences approach $a$: rational sequences and irrational sequences. For continuity we need every sequence to give the same limit $f(a).$
  • A rational sequence $(r_n)\to a$ forces $f(r_n)=r_n\to a.$
  • An irrational sequence $(s_n)\to a$ forces $f(s_n)=1-s_n\to 1-a.$
For both limits to agree with $f(a)$ we need $a=1-a,$ i.e., $a=1/2.$ So the only candidate for continuity is $a=1/2.$ We then verify separately: $f$ is continuous at $1/2$ and discontinuous everywhere else.
Claim — $f$ is discontinuous at every $a\ne\tfrac{1}{2}$
If $a\ne\tfrac{1}{2},$ then $a\ne 1-a,$ so by density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R},$ the two types of sequences converging to $a$ produce different image limits — neither equals $f(a)$ in general.
Proof — Discontinuity at $a\ne\tfrac{1}{2}.$ By density, there exist sequences $(r_n)$ in $\mathbb{Q}$ and $(s_n)$ in $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q},$ both converging to $a.$

Rational sequence: $f(r_n)=r_n\to a.$
Irrational sequence: $f(s_n)=1-s_n\to 1-a.$

Since $a\ne\tfrac{1}{2},$ we have $a\ne 1-a.$ So these two sequences converge to $a$ but give $f$-values tending to two different limits $a$ and $1-a.$ By uniqueness of limits, $f$ cannot be continuous at $a.$ $\blacksquare$
Claim — $f$ is continuous at $a=\tfrac{1}{2}$
At $x=\tfrac{1}{2}:$ both branches give the same value: $f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (since $\tfrac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}$) and $1-\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}.$ So $f(x)\to\tfrac{1}{2}$ along any sequence tending to $\tfrac{1}{2}.$
Proof — Continuity at $a=\tfrac{1}{2}.$ Note $f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (since $\tfrac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}$). Let $(x_n)$ be any sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ with $x_n\to\tfrac{1}{2}.$

For each $n,$ exactly one of two cases holds:
  • If $x_n\in\mathbb{Q}$: $f(x_n)=x_n.$
  • If $x_n\notin\mathbb{Q}$: $f(x_n)=1-x_n.$
In either case, $$\left|f(x_n)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right| =\begin{cases}|x_n-\tfrac{1}{2}| & x_n\in\mathbb{Q},\\ |1-x_n-\tfrac{1}{2}|=|x_n-\tfrac{1}{2}| & x_n\notin\mathbb{Q}.\end{cases}$$ Hence $\left|f(x_n)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|=\left|x_n-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|\to 0.$ By the Sandwich Theorem, $f(x_n)\to\tfrac{1}{2}=f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right).$ Since $(x_n)$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous at $\tfrac{1}{2}.$ $\blacksquare$
The two branches $y=x$ (rational, blue) and $y=1-x$ (irrational, red) are both drawn as lines, but each is only "sampled" on a dense subset. They meet at exactly one point $\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$ — the unique point of continuity.
Key Observation
Notice: $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=1-x$ are both continuous functions on $\mathbb{R}.$ They agree precisely when $x=1-x,$ i.e., at $x=\tfrac{1}{2}.$ The piecewise function $f$ is continuous exactly at the points where its two "branch functions" have equal values — which is only $x=\tfrac{1}{2}.$

This is a general principle: a function defined piecewise by continuous branch functions can be continuous at a point $a$ only if all branches agree at $a.$
Conclusion
$f$ is continuous at exactly one point: $x=\tfrac{1}{2}.$
$f$ is discontinuous at every $x\ne\tfrac{1}{2}.$

The set of continuity is the singleton $\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr\}.$

Example 3 — A Piecewise Function Continuous Everywhere

The Piecewise Function
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x\ge 0\\ x & x<0. \end{cases}$$ Claim: $f$ is continuous at every $a\in\mathbb{R}.$
The graph is connected at the origin: $f(x)=x$ (left branch) meets $f(x)=x^2$ (right branch) smoothly at $(0,0).$ Both branches arrive at $0$ as $x\to 0.$
Proof — Continuity at $a\ne 0.$ Case $a>0$: There exists $N$ such that $x_k>0$ for all $k\ge N.$ So $f(x_k)=x_k^2\to a^2=f(a).$ ✓

Case $a<0$: There exists $N$ such that $x_k<0$ for all $k\ge N.$ So $f(x_k)=x_k\to a=f(a).$ ✓ $\blacksquare$
Proof — Continuity at $a=0$ (the critical case). $f(0)=0^2=0.$ Let $(x_n)$ be any sequence with $x_n\to 0.$ We must show $f(x_n)\to 0.$

Each $x_n$ is either $\ge 0$ (giving $f(x_n)=x_n^2$) or $<0$ (giving $f(x_n)=x_n$). In both cases $|f(x_n)|\le|x_n|$ for $|x_n|\le 1$:
  • If $x_n\ge 0$: $|f(x_n)|=x_n^2=|x_n|^2\le|x_n|$ (since $|x_n|\le 1$).
  • If $x_n<0$: $|f(x_n)|=|x_n|.$
Since $x_n\to 0,$ there exists $N_0$ such that $|x_k|\le 1$ for all $k\ge N_0.$ For such $k$: $0\le|f(x_k)|\le|x_k|.$

Since $x_n\to 0$ iff $|x_n|\to 0,$ the Sandwich Lemma gives $|f(x_n)|\to 0,$ hence $f(x_n)\to 0 = f(0).$ $\blacksquare$

Generalisation to $f(x) = x^m$ for $x\ge 0$ and $f(x) = x^n$ for $x < 0$

Generalised Piecewise Power Function
Let $m,n\in\mathbb{N}$ and define $$g(x) = \begin{cases} x^m & x\ge 0\\ x^n & x<0. \end{cases}$$
  • At any $a\ne 0$: continuous by sign persistence (same argument as above).
  • At $a=0$: $g(0)=0.$ For $x_n\to 0$:
    • $x_n\ge 0$: $|g(x_n)|=|x_n|^m\le|x_n|$ once $|x_n|\le 1.$
    • $x_n<0$ and $n$ odd: $|g(x_n)|=|x_n|^n\le|x_n|$ once $|x_n|\le 1.$
    • $x_n<0$ and $n$ even: $g(x_n)=|x_n|^n\ge 0$; same bound.
    In all cases $|g(x_n)|\le|x_n|\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $g(x_n)\to 0=g(0).$ ✓
Conclusion: $g$ is continuous at $0$ (and everywhere) for any $m,n\in\mathbb{N}.$

Example 4 — $f(x)=1/x$ is Continuous on $\mathbb{R}^*$

Proposition
Let $\mathbb{R}^*=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.$ Define $f:\mathbb{R}^*\to\mathbb{R}^*$ by $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x}.$ Then $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^*.$
$y=1/x$: continuous on $(0,\infty)$ and on $(-\infty,0).$ The "gap" at $x=0$ is not a discontinuity — $0$ is simply not in the domain.
Proof. Let $a\in\mathbb{R}^*$ (so $a\ne 0$) and let $(x_n)$ be any sequence in $\mathbb{R}^*$ with $x_n\to a.$

Since $x_n\to a\ne 0,$ by sign persistence there exists $N_0$ such that $x_k\ne 0$ for all $k\ge N_0$ (in fact, $x_k$ has the same sign as $a$). So $1/x_n$ is eventually defined.

By the algebra of convergent sequences (quotient rule): $$f(x_n) = \frac{1}{x_n} \;\to\; \frac{1}{a} = f(a),$$ since $x_n\to a$ and $a\ne 0.$

Since $(x_n)$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous at $a.$ Since $a\in\mathbb{R}^*$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^*.$ $\blacksquare$
Remark on the domain
Continuity is always with respect to the domain of $f.$ Asking whether $f(x)=1/x$ is continuous at $0$ is meaningless — $f$ is simply not defined at $0.$ The function is continuous everywhere it is defined.

Structural Theorems

Theorem A — Two Continuous Functions Agreeing on $\mathbb{Q}$ Must Be Equal

Theorem
Let $f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous. If $f(x)=g(x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb{Q},$ then $f=g$ on all of $\mathbb{R}.$
Proof. Let $a\in\mathbb{R}.$ By the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R},$ there exists a sequence of rationals $(r_n)$ with $r_n\to a.$

Since $r_n\in\mathbb{Q}$ for all $n$ and $f(r_n)=g(r_n)$ for all $n$ (by hypothesis):
By continuity of $f$: $f(r_n)\to f(a).$
By continuity of $g$: $g(r_n)\to g(a).$

But $f(r_n)=g(r_n)$ for all $n,$ so both sequences are identical. By uniqueness of limits: $f(a)=g(a).$

Since $a\in\mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, $f=g$ on $\mathbb{R}.$ $\blacksquare$
Interpretation. A continuous function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is completely determined by its values on $\mathbb{Q}$ — a countable set. This is because $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ and continuity forces the values at irrational points to be limits of values at nearby rationals.

In other words: you cannot have two different continuous functions that agree at every rational number.
A useful corollary
If $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $f(r)=0$ for every $r\in\mathbb{Q},$ then $f\equiv 0.$

Proof: Apply the theorem with $g\equiv 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Theorem B — Continuous Additive Homomorphisms are Linear

Theorem
Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous and satisfy $$f(x+y) = f(x)+f(y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.$$ Then $f(x)=\lambda x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R},$ where $\lambda=f(1).$
Proof. Step 1 — Values at integers. Setting $x=y=0$: $f(0)=f(0)+f(0),$ so $f(0)=0.$
For $n\in\mathbb{N}$ by induction: $f(n)=nf(1)=n\lambda.$
Setting $y=-x$: $f(0)=f(x)+f(-x),$ so $f(-x)=-f(x).$ Hence $f(-n)=-n\lambda$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}.$ So $f(n)=n\lambda$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}.$

Step 2 — Values at rationals. For $p/q\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $q\in\mathbb{N}$: $$f(p) = f\!\left(\underbrace{\frac{p}{q}+\frac{p}{q}+\cdots+\frac{p}{q}}_{q\text{ times}}\right) = q\,f\!\left(\frac{p}{q}\right).$$ So $f\!\left(\dfrac{p}{q}\right)=\dfrac{f(p)}{q}=\dfrac{p\lambda}{q}=\lambda\cdot\dfrac{p}{q}.$

Hence $f(r)=\lambda r$ for all $r\in\mathbb{Q}.$

Step 3 — Extension to all reals by continuity. Define $g(x)=\lambda x.$ Then $g$ is continuous and $f(r)=g(r)$ for all $r\in\mathbb{Q}.$ By Theorem A, $f=g$ on all of $\mathbb{R}.$ Hence $f(x)=\lambda x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}.$ $\blacksquare$
Without the continuity assumption — the Cauchy equation $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ has infinitely many strange solutions (using Hamel bases of $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, whose existence requires the Axiom of Choice). These pathological solutions are unbounded on every interval and nowhere continuous. Continuity alone forces linearity.

Self-Assessment — Part A: Basic Questions

Q1. State precisely what it means for $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ to be discontinuous at $a$ using the sequential definition. Write out the negation symbol by symbol.

Answer. $f$ is discontinuous at $a$ if: $$\exists\,(x_n)\text{ in }D \text{ with }x_n\to a \text{ such that }f(x_n)\not\to f(a).$$ Spelling out "$f(x_n)\not\to f(a)$": $\exists\,\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $N\in\mathbb{N},$ $\exists\,n>N$ with $|f(x_n)-f(a)|\ge\varepsilon_0.$

Q2. Show that the function $f(x)=\begin{cases}x&x\in\mathbb{Q}\\0&x\notin\mathbb{Q}\end{cases}$ is continuous at $0$ and discontinuous at every $a\ne 0.$

Proof. At $0$: $f(0)=0.$ For any $x_n\to 0$: $|f(x_n)|\le|x_n|\to 0$ (since $|f(x_n)|=|x_n|$ if rational, $=0$ if irrational). By Sandwich, $f(x_n)\to 0=f(0).$ ✓

At $a\ne 0$ rational: Choose irrationals $s_n\to a.$ Then $f(s_n)=0\not\to a=f(a).$

At $a\ne 0$ irrational: Choose rationals $r_n\to a.$ Then $f(r_n)=r_n\to a\ne 0=f(a).$ $\blacksquare$

Q3. Let $f(x)=\begin{cases}x+1&x\ge 0\\x-1&x<0.\end{cases}$ Is $f$ continuous at $0$? At $a=1$?

Answer. At $a=0$: $f(0)=1.$ Take $x_n=-1/n\to 0.$ Then $x_n<0,$ so $f(x_n)=x_n-1=-1/n-1\to -1\ne 1=f(0).$ Discontinuous at $0.$ ✗

At $a=1$: $a=1>0,$ so by sign persistence, eventually $x_k>0$ for any $x_k\to 1.$ Then $f(x_k)=x_k+1\to 1+1=2=f(1).$ Continuous at $1.$ ✓

Q4. Using the sequential definition, prove directly that $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous at every $a\in\mathbb{R}.$

Proof. Let $x_n\to a.$ By the product rule for sequence limits: $f(x_n)=x_n^2=x_n\cdot x_n\to a\cdot a=a^2=f(a).$ $\blacksquare$

Alternatively: $|x_n^2-a^2|=|x_n-a||x_n+a|\le|x_n-a|(|x_n|+|a|).$ Since $(x_n)$ is convergent it is bounded: $|x_n|\le M.$ So $|x_n^2-a^2|\le(M+|a|)|x_n-a|\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q5. If $J=\{a\},$ then any function from $J\to \mathbb{R}$ is continous.

Proof. It is clear that every such function constant. $\blacksquare$

Q6.

  1. If $J=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\},$ then any function from $J\to \mathbb{R}$ is continous.
  2. If $J=\mathbb{N}$ then any function from $J\to \mathbb{R}$ is continous.
  3. If $J\subset \mathbb{R},$ suth that $min\{\{x,y\}\subset J: |x-y|\ge \delta>0\}$ then any function from $J\to \mathbb{R}$ is continous.

Proof of Part-1 (Remaining are similar). It is clear that every such function eventually constant.
Let $a\in J.$
Without loss of genrality assume $a=a_1.$
Consider $S=\{|a-a_i|: i\in \{2,3,\ldots n\}\}.$ Let $min(S)=\delta.$ If we choose $\epsilon=\delta/3$ $x_n\to a$ forces all $x_n$ after some stage is equal to $a.$ $\blacksquare$

Q7. Prove: if $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $a$ and $f(a)>0,$ then there is an open interval $(a-\delta,a+\delta)$ on which $f>0.$ (Hint: suppose not and construct a sequence.)

Proof. Suppose no such $\delta$ exists. Then for each $n\in\mathbb{N},$ taking $\delta=1/n,$ there exists $x_n$ with $|x_n-a|<1/n$ and $f(x_n)\le 0.$
Since $|x_n-a|<1/n\to 0,$ we have $x_n\to a.$ By continuity: $f(x_n)\to f(a)>0.$ But $f(x_n)\le 0$ for all $n,$ so $\lim f(x_n)\le 0$ — contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Self-Assessment — Part B: Deeper Questions (do not do now)

Q8. Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous with $f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}$ for all $x.$ Prove $f$ is constant.

Proof. Suppose $f(a)\ne f(b)$ for some $a,b.$ WLOG $f(a)<f(b).$ By the Intermediate Value Theorem (which follows from sequential continuity), $f$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ — in particular some irrational value, since $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in every interval. But this contradicts $f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}$ for all $x.$ Hence $f$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

Alternative without IVT: Suppose not constant. Then there exist $a,b$ with $f(a)\ne f(b).$ Define $g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ by $g(t)=f((1-t)a+tb)-f(a).$ Then $g$ is continuous, $g(0)=0$ and $g(1)=f(b)-f(a)\ne 0.$ By IVT applied to $g,$ $g$ takes irrational values — contradiction.

Q9. Suppose $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfies $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ for all $x,y.$ Without assuming continuity, prove: (a) $f(0)=0;$ (b) $f(-x)=-f(x);$ (c) $f(nx)=nf(x)$ for $n\in\mathbb{Z};$ (d) $f(p/q)=(p/q)f(1)$ for $p/q\in\mathbb{Q}.$

Proof. (a) $f(0)=f(0+0)=f(0)+f(0)\Rightarrow f(0)=0.$
(b) $0=f(0)=f(x+(-x))=f(x)+f(-x)\Rightarrow f(-x)=-f(x).$
(c) Induction: $f(nx)=f((n-1)x+x)=f((n-1)x)+f(x)=nf(x).$ For negative $n$: $f(-nx)=-f(nx)=-nf(x),$ so $f(nx)=nf(x).$
(d) $qf(p/q)=f(q\cdot p/q)=f(p)=pf(1),$ so $f(p/q)=(p/q)f(1).$ $\blacksquare$

Q10. The Dirichlet function $f=1_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is nowhere continuous. Is $f+g$ continuous anywhere if $g(x)=1-1_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)$? What is $f+g$?

Answer. $g(x)=\begin{cases}0&x\in\mathbb{Q}\\1&x\notin\mathbb{Q}\end{cases}$ is also nowhere continuous (same Dirichlet argument).

$(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)=1$ for all $x$ — the constant function $1,$ which is continuous everywhere! ✓

Moral: the sum of two nowhere-continuous functions can be everywhere continuous.

Q11. Define $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2\sin(1/x)&x\ne 0\\0&x=0.\end{cases}$ Is $f$ continuous at $0$? Is $f$ differentiable at $0$?

Answer. Continuous at $0$: $|f(x_n)-f(0)|=|x_n^2\sin(1/x_n)|\le x_n^2\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $f(x_n)\to 0=f(0).$ ✓

Differentiable at $0$: $\dfrac{f(x)-f(0)}{x-0}=\dfrac{x^2\sin(1/x)}{x}=x\sin(1/x).$ As $x\to 0$: $|x\sin(1/x)|\le|x|\to 0,$ so $f'(0)=0.$ ✓

$f$ is both continuous and differentiable at $0,$ but $f'$ is not continuous at $0$ (since $f'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)$ for $x\ne 0$ oscillates wildly near $0$).

Q12. Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous with $f(f(x))=x$ for all $x.$ Prove $f$ is strictly monotone. (Hint: show $f$ is injective, then use the IVT to conclude monotonicity.)

Proof sketch. Injectivity: If $f(a)=f(b),$ apply $f$ to both sides: $a=f(f(a))=f(f(b))=b.$ So $f$ is injective.

Monotonicity: A continuous injective function $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ must be strictly monotone. (If not, there exist $a<b<c$ with $f(b)$ not between $f(a)$ and $f(c)$; then the IVT applied to $f$ on $[a,b]$ and $[b,c]$ gives a repeated value, contradicting injectivity.) $\blacksquare$

Q13 (Challenge). A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is called Lipschitz with constant $L$ if $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}.$ Prove every Lipschitz function is continuous. Show the converse fails: $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ is continuous but not Lipschitz.

Proof. Lipschitz $\Rightarrow$ continuous: If $x_n\to a,$ then $|f(x_n)-f(a)|\le L|x_n-a|\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

$\sqrt{x}$ not Lipschitz on $[0,1]$: If it were: $|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{0}|\le L|x-0|,$ i.e., $\sqrt{x}\le Lx,$ i.e., $1/\sqrt{x}\le L$ for all $x\in(0,1].$ But $1/\sqrt{x}\to\infty$ as $x\to 0^+.$ Contradiction.

$\sqrt{x}$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ (proved earlier by rationalization) but not Lipschitz.
