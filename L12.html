The Sandwich Theorem for Sequences

Statement, proof, five canonical applications, further worked examples, the Nested Interval Theorem, and self-assessment.

1. The Sandwich (Squeeze) Theorem

Theorem — Sandwich Lemma for Sequences
Let $(a_n),\,(x_n),\,(b_n)$ be real sequences satisfying $$a_n \;\le\; x_n \;\le\; b_n \qquad \text{for all } n \ge N_0$$ for some fixed $N_0\in\mathbb{N}.$ If $a_n\to L$ and $b_n\to L$ (the same limit $L$), then $$x_n \;\longrightarrow\; L.$$
Intuition. $(x_n)$ is sandwiched between $(a_n)$ (floor) and $(b_n)$ (ceiling). As both the floor and ceiling squeeze toward $L,$ the middle sequence has nowhere to go but $L$ as well.
Both the upper bound $(b_n)$ and lower bound $(a_n)$ converge to $L.$ The middle sequence $(x_n)$ is trapped between them and must also converge to $L.$
Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0.$

Since $a_n\to L,$ there exists $N_1\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $n\ge N_1\Rightarrow|a_n-L|<\varepsilon,$ i.e., $L-\varepsilon<a_n<L+\varepsilon.$
Since $b_n\to L,$ there exists $N_2\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $n\ge N_2\Rightarrow|b_n-L|<\varepsilon,$ i.e., $L-\varepsilon<b_n<L+\varepsilon.$

Set $N=\max(N_0,N_1,N_2).$ For all $n\ge N$: $$L-\varepsilon \;<\; a_n \;\le\; x_n \;\le\; b_n \;<\; L+\varepsilon.$$ Hence $|x_n-L|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N.$ Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $x_n\to L.$ $\blacksquare$
Important Remarks
  • The inequality $a_n\le x_n\le b_n$ need hold only for all sufficiently large $n$ (i.e., beyond some $N_0$). Finitely many exceptions do not affect convergence.
  • Both bounds must converge to the same limit $L.$ If they converge to different values, the theorem says nothing.
  • The theorem gives convergence but does not require us to guess the limit in advance — the limit is automatically identified as $L.$

2. Applications — Part I

Application 1 — Squeezing Any Sequence Toward a Point

Proposition
Let $a\in\mathbb{R}.$ For each $n\in\mathbb{N},$ select any element $x_n\in\!\left(a-\tfrac{1}{n},\,a+\tfrac{1}{n}\right).$ Then $x_n\to a.$
Proof. By definition of $x_n$: $$a - \frac{1}{n} \;<\; x_n \;<\; a + \frac{1}{n}.$$ The sequences $\left(a-\tfrac{1}{n}\right)$ and $\left(a+\tfrac{1}{n}\right)$ both converge to $a$ (by the algebra of limits: constant $a$ plus $\pm 1/n\to 0$). By the Sandwich Theorem, $x_n\to a.$ $\blacksquare$
Why this matters. This is one of the most frequently used observations in real analysis. It says: to produce a sequence converging to $a,$ just pick one element from each shrinking interval around $a$ — no explicit formula is needed. We use it repeatedly to prove density results and to construct sequences with prescribed limits.
Shrinking open intervals $(a-1/n,\,a+1/n)$ around $a.$ Any choice $x_n$ from each interval is squeezed to $a.$

Application 2 — $\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\to 0$

Proposition
$\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sin n}{n} = 0.$
Proof. Since $-1\le\sin n\le 1$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N},$ dividing by $n>0$ preserves the inequality: $$-\frac{1}{n} \;\le\; \frac{\sin n}{n} \;\le\; \frac{1}{n}.$$ Both $-1/n$ and $1/n$ converge to $0.$ By the Sandwich Theorem, $\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$
Key pattern. When a bounded sequence is multiplied by a sequence tending to $0,$ the product tends to $0.$ Here $|\sin n|\le 1$ and $1/n\to 0.$ This is a recurring Sandwich pattern: $$ -\frac{B}{n}\le\frac{\text{bounded}}{n}\le\frac{B}{n}\;\to\;0.$$
$(\sin n)/n$ oscillates but is trapped between $\pm 1/n\to 0.$ Both bounds squeeze it to $0.$

3. Applications — Part II: Density and Least Upper Bounds

Application 3 — Sequences Converging to Any Real Number

Proposition
For every real number $x,$ there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ of real numbers with $x_n\to x.$
Proof. For each $n\in\mathbb{N},$ choose any $x_n$ satisfying $x-\dfrac{1}{n}<x_n<x.$ (Such $x_n$ exists: e.g. take $x_n=x-\dfrac{1}{2n}.$)

Then $x-\dfrac{1}{n}<x_n<x,$ so by the Sandwich Theorem (with bounds $x-1/n\to x$ and constant $x\to x$): $x_n\to x.$ $\blacksquare$

Application 4 — Rational and Irrational Sequences Converging to Any Real

Proposition — Density via Sandwich
For every $x\in\mathbb{R}:$
  • There exists a sequence $(r_n)$ of rationals with $r_n\to x.$
  • There exists a sequence $(t_n)$ of irrationals with $t_n\to x.$
Proof — Rational sequence. For each $n\in\mathbb{N},$ the interval $\left(x-\dfrac{1}{n},\,x\right)$ is nonempty. By the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R},$ there exists a rational $r_n$ with $$x - \frac{1}{n} \;<\; r_n \;<\; x.$$ Both bounds converge to $x.$ By the Sandwich Theorem, $r_n\to x.$ $\blacksquare$
Proof — Irrational sequence. For each $n,$ by the density of irrationals, there exists an irrational $t_n$ with $$x - \frac{1}{n} \;<\; t_n \;<\; x.$$ Again $t_n\to x$ by Sandwich. $\blacksquare$
Why Application 4 Matters
This is the fundamental tool for proving results about continuity: at any point $a,$ we can approach from the rationals and from the irrationals. For example, the Dirichlet function is discontinuous everywhere precisely because these two types of approaching sequences give different $f$-values.

Application 5 — The LUB is the Limit of a Sequence in the Set

Proposition
Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be nonempty and bounded above, and let $\alpha=\text{lub}(A).$ Then there exists a sequence $(a_n)$ with $a_n\in A$ for all $n$ and $a_n\to\alpha.$

Analogously: if $\beta=\text{glb}(A),$ there exists a sequence $(b_n)$ with $b_n\in A$ and $b_n\to\beta.$
Proof. Since $\alpha=\text{lub}(A),$ for each $n\in\mathbb{N}$ the number $\alpha-\dfrac{1}{n}$ is not an upper bound of $A$ (as it is strictly less than $\alpha$). Hence there exists $a_n\in A$ with $$\alpha - \frac{1}{n} \;<\; a_n \;\le\; \alpha.$$ By the Sandwich Theorem (with bounds $\alpha-1/n\to\alpha$ and $\alpha\to\alpha$): $a_n\to\alpha.$ $\blacksquare$
Elements $a_n\in A$ satisfying $\alpha-1/n<a_n\le\alpha$ are sandwiched between $\alpha-1/n\to\alpha$ and $\alpha\to\alpha,$ so $a_n\to\alpha.$
GLB Version
If $\beta=\text{glb}(A),$ then $\beta+\tfrac{1}{n}$ is not a lower bound for $A,$ so there exists $b_n\in A$ with $$\beta \;\le\; b_n \;<\; \beta+\frac{1}{n}.$$ By Sandwich, $b_n\to\beta.$ $\blacksquare$

4. Further Applications

Application 6 — Bounded Times Zero: $a_n x_n\to 0$

Proposition
Let $(a_n)$ be bounded (i.e. $|a_n|\le B$ for some $B>0$ and all $n$) and let $x_n\to 0.$ Then $a_n x_n\to 0.$
Proof. Since $|a_n|\le B$ for all $n$: $$-B|x_n| \;\le\; a_n x_n \;\le\; B|x_n|.$$ Since $x_n\to 0,$ we have $|x_n|\to 0,$ so $B|x_n|\to 0$ and $-B|x_n|\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $a_n x_n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$
Examples
  • $\sin n\cdot\dfrac{1}{n}\to 0$   ($|\sin n|\le 1$, $1/n\to 0$)
  • $(-1)^n\cdot\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0$   ($|(-1)^n|=1$, $1/\sqrt{n}\to 0$)
  • $\cos(n^2)\cdot r^n\to 0$ for $|r|<1$   ($|\cos(n^2)|\le 1$, $r^n\to 0$)

Application 7 — $\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}\to 0$

Proposition
$\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\!\left(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}\right)=0.$
Proof. Rationalise by multiplying by the conjugate: $$\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n} = \frac{(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})(\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}} = \frac{(n+1)-n}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}}.$$ Since $\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}\ge 2\sqrt{n},$ we have: $$0 \;\le\; \sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}.$$ Since $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\to 0,$ the Sandwich Theorem gives $\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Application 8 — A Telescoping Sum $\to 1$

Proposition
Let $x_n = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+2}}+\cdots+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}}.$ Then $x_n\to 1.$
Proof. The sum $x_n$ has exactly $n$ terms. Since the function $t\mapsto 1/\sqrt{t}$ is decreasing, and $n^2+1\le n^2+k\le n^2+n$ for $k=1,\ldots,n$: $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+k}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}.$$ Summing over $k=1,\ldots,n$ (each side $n$ terms): $$\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \;\le\; x_n \;\le\; \frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}.$$ Now compute the limits of the bounds: $$\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n}} \;\to\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0}} = 1,$$ $$\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n^2}} \;\to\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0}} = 1.$$ By Sandwich, $x_n\to 1.$ $\blacksquare$
The pattern: For sums of $n$ terms each of size roughly $1/n,$ the total is roughly $1.$ The Sandwich Theorem makes this precise by bounding each term above and below by the largest and smallest term.

Application 9 — $(a^n+b^n)^{1/n}\to b$ for $0<a<b$

Proposition
Let $0<a<b.$ Then $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(a^n+b^n)^{1/n}=b.$
Proof. Since $0<a<b$: $b^n\le a^n+b^n\le 2b^n.$ Taking $n$-th roots (which preserves inequalities for positive quantities): $$b \;=\; (b^n)^{1/n} \;\le\; (a^n+b^n)^{1/n} \;\le\; (2b^n)^{1/n} = 2^{1/n}\cdot b.$$ Since $2^{1/n}\to 1$ (proved in the Important Limits lecture), the right bound $2^{1/n}\cdot b\to 1\cdot b=b.$ The left bound is the constant sequence $b.$ By Sandwich, $(a^n+b^n)^{1/n}\to b.$ $\blacksquare$

5. The Nested Interval Theorem

Theorem — Nested Interval Theorem (Standard Version)
For each $n\in\mathbb{N},$ let $J_n=[a_n,b_n]$ be a closed bounded interval. Suppose the intervals are nested: $$J_1\supseteq J_2\supseteq J_3\supseteq\cdots$$ (equivalently: $a_n\le a_{n+1}$ and $b_{n+1}\le b_n$ for all $n$).

Assume further that $b_n-a_n\to 0$ (the lengths go to zero).

Then there exists a unique $c$ such that $\displaystyle\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}J_n=\{c\}.$
Nested closed intervals $J_1\supseteq J_2\supseteq\cdots$ with lengths $b_n-a_n\to 0.$ Their intersection collapses to a single point $c.$ $(a_n)$ increases and $(b_n)$ decreases, both converging to $c.$
Proof. Existence. The sequence $(a_n)$ is monotone increasing (since $J_{n+1}\subseteq J_n$ implies $a_n\le a_{n+1}$) and bounded above by $b_1.$ The sequence $(b_n)$ is monotone decreasing and bounded below by $a_1.$ By the Monotone Convergence Theorem: $$\alpha = \text{lub}\{a_n\} = \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \beta = \text{glb}\{b_n\} = \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n$$ both exist. Since $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n,$ passing to limits gives $\alpha\le\beta.$

Any $c\in[\alpha,\beta]$ satisfies $a_n\le\alpha\le c\le\beta\le b_n$ for all $n,$ so $c\in J_n$ for all $n,$ hence $c\in\bigcap_n J_n.$

Uniqueness. Let $c,d\in\bigcap_n J_n.$ Assume $c\le d.$ Since $a_n\le c\le d\le b_n$: $$0 \;\le\; d-c \;\le\; b_n-a_n.$$ Since $b_n-a_n\to 0,$ the Sandwich Theorem gives $d-c=0,$ i.e., $d=c.$

So $\bigcap_n J_n$ contains exactly one point, call it $c=\alpha=\beta.$ $\blacksquare$
Why "closed" intervals are essential
The theorem fails for open intervals. Example: $J_n=\left(0,\tfrac{1}{n}\right).$ These are nested and their lengths $1/n\to 0,$ yet $\displaystyle\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\!\left(0,\tfrac{1}{n}\right)=\emptyset$ (any $x>0$ is eventually excluded, and $0$ is not in any $J_n$). The endpoint $0$ is the limit but is never included!
The Sandwich argument at the heart of uniqueness. The key line is $0\le d-c\le b_n-a_n\to 0,$ giving $d-c=0$ by Sandwich. This is the Sandwich Theorem applied to the constant sequence $0$ and the sequence $b_n-a_n\to 0,$ with $d-c$ as the "middle" (constant) sequence.

1. Theorem — Six Important Limits

Theorem — Important Limits
  1. Let $0\le r<1$ and $x_n:=r^n.$ Then $x_n\to 0.$
  2. Let $-1<t<1.$ Then $t^n\to 0.$
  3. Let $|r|<1.$ Then $nr^n\to 0.$
  4. Let $a>0.$ Then $a^{1/n}\to 1.$
  5. $n^{1/n}\to 1.$
  6. Fix $a\in\mathbb{R}.$ Then $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\to 0.$
Why these limits matter. These are the building blocks of most convergence arguments in real analysis. Limits (1)–(3) control geometric decay; limits (4)–(5) show that $n$-th roots always normalise to $1$; limit (6) shows that factorials grow faster than any exponential.

2. Proof of (1): $r^n\to 0$ for $0\le r<1$

Key Tool — Bernoulli's Inequality
For $h>-1$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$: $$(1+h)^n \;\ge\; 1+nh.$$ Proof by induction: base $n=1$ clear; step $(1+h)^{k+1}=(1+h)^k(1+h)\ge(1+kh)(1+h)=1+(k+1)h+kh^2\ge 1+(k+1)h.$
✏ Strategy For $r=0$: trivial. For $0<r<1$: write $r=\dfrac{1}{1+h}$ where $h=\dfrac{1-r}{r}>0.$ Then $r^n=\dfrac{1}{(1+h)^n}.$ Bernoulli gives $(1+h)^n\ge 1+nh\ge nh,$ so $0\le r^n\le\dfrac{1}{nh}\to 0$ by the Sandwich Lemma.
Proof. The case $r=0$: $x_n=0$ for all $n,$ so $x_n\to 0.$ ✓

Let $0<r<1.$ Set $h=\dfrac{1}{r}-1=\dfrac{1-r}{r}>0.$ Then $r=\dfrac{1}{1+h}$ and $r^n=\dfrac{1}{(1+h)^n}.$

By Bernoulli's Inequality: $$(1+h)^n \;\ge\; 1+nh \;\ge\; nh.$$ Therefore $$0 \;\le\; r^n = \frac{1}{(1+h)^n} \;\le\; \frac{1}{nh}.$$ Since $\dfrac{1}{nh}\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$ (fixed $h>0$), the Sandwich Lemma gives $r^n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$
$r^n$ for $r=0.2,0.5,0.9.$ All converge to $0;$ smaller $r$ converges faster.
Proof of (2): $t^n\to 0$ for $-1<t<1$
$|t^n - 0| = |t|^n = |t|^n.$ Since $|t|\in[0,1),$ by (1) we have $|t|^n\to 0.$ Hence $t^n\to 0$ by the criterion $x_n\to 0\iff|x_n|\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

3. Proof of (3): $nr^n\to 0$ for $|r|<1$

Why the estimate from (1) is insufficient
Using (1) with $r\in(0,1)$: $r^n\le\dfrac{1}{nh}$ gives $nr^n\le\dfrac{1}{h},$ a constant — useless for showing $nr^n\to 0.$
We need a sharper bound, obtained by keeping the quadratic term in the Binomial expansion.
✏ Strategy — Keep the $h^2$ term Write $r=\dfrac{1}{1+h}$ with $h>0.$ Use the Binomial Theorem: $$(1+h)^n \;\ge\; \binom{n}{2}h^2 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}h^2.$$ So $r^n\le\dfrac{2}{n(n-1)h^2}$ and $nr^n\le\dfrac{2}{(n-1)h^2}\to 0.$
Proof (for $0<r<1$). It suffices to prove the result for $0<r<1$ (the case $r=0$ is trivial; $-1<r<0$ follows since $|nr^n|=n|r|^n$).

Write $r=\dfrac{1}{1+h}$ with $h=\dfrac{1-r}{r}>0.$ By the Binomial Theorem, for $n\ge 2$: $$(1+h)^n \;=\; 1+nh+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}h^2+\cdots \;\ge\; \frac{n(n-1)}{2}h^2.$$ All terms are positive so we keep only the $k=2$ term. Therefore: $$0 \;\le\; nr^n = \frac{n}{(1+h)^n} \;\le\; \frac{n}{\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}h^2} = \frac{2}{(n-1)h^2}.$$ Since $\dfrac{2}{(n-1)h^2}\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$ (fixed $h>0$), the Sandwich Lemma gives $nr^n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$
Remark: the Divide and Conquer trick. Here is an alternative proof using the method described at the end of the textbook images. For $0<r<1,$ choose $N$ such that $r<1/N^{1/N}$ (possible since $N^{1/N}\to 1$). Split the factors of $r^n$ into two groups. The factorials proof below uses the same philosophy.
$nr^n$: increases to a peak (the $n$ factor wins initially) then decays to $0$ (the $r^n$ factor eventually dominates). Both curves converge to $0.$

4. Proofs of (4) $a^{1/n}\to 1$ and (5) $n^{1/n}\to 1$

Proof of (4): $a^{1/n}\to 1$ for $a>0$

The three cases
Case $a=1$: $a^{1/n}=1$ always. ✓

Case $a>1$: Write $a^{1/n}=1+h_n$ where $h_n>0$ (since $a^{1/n}>1$ when $a>1$). By Bernoulli: $a=(1+h_n)^n\ge 1+nh_n,$ so $h_n\le\dfrac{a-1}{n}\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $h_n\to 0,$ hence $a^{1/n}\to 1.$

Case $0<a<1$: Set $b=1/a>1.$ By the case above, $b^{1/n}\to 1.$ Then $a^{1/n}=1/b^{1/n}\to 1/1=1.$ ✓
Formal Proof (case $a>1$). Since $a>1,$ we have $a^{1/n}>1.$ Write $a^{1/n}=1+h_n$ with $h_n>0.$ Then $a=(1+h_n)^n\ge 1+nh_n$ by Bernoulli's Inequality. So $h_n\le\dfrac{a-1}{n}.$ Since $0\le h_n\le\dfrac{a-1}{n}\to 0,$ the Sandwich Lemma gives $h_n\to 0.$ Hence $a^{1/n}=1+h_n\to 1.$ $\blacksquare$

Proof of (5): $n^{1/n}\to 1$

✏ Strategy — Use the quadratic Binomial term Write $n^{1/n}=1+h_n$ with $h_n\ge 0$ for $n\ge 1.$ Need $h_n\to 0.$ Cube the Binomial: $(1+h_n)^n\ge\binom{n}{2}h_n^2=\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}h_n^2.$ So $n\ge\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}h_n^2,$ giving $h_n^2\le\dfrac{2}{n-1}\to 0,$ hence $h_n\to 0.$
Proof. For $n=1$: $n^{1/n}=1.$ For $n\ge 2$: $n^{1/n}>1,$ so write $n^{1/n}=1+h_n$ with $h_n>0.$

Using the Binomial Theorem (keeping only the $k=2$ term, all others positive): $$n=(1+h_n)^n \;\ge\; \binom{n}{2}h_n^2 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}h_n^2.$$ Dividing by $n>0$: $$h_n^2 \;\le\; \frac{2}{n-1}.$$ Since $h_n\ge 0$: $0\le h_n\le\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{n-1}}\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $h_n\to 0,$ so $n^{1/n}=1+h_n\to 1.$ $\blacksquare$
Connection between (4) and (5)
For $a\ge 1$: since $a^{1/n}\le n^{1/n}$ when $n\ge a,$ and both sides $\to 1$ as $n\to\infty,$ the Sandwich Lemma gives $a^{1/n}\to 1$ as an alternative to the proof above.

Also: $1\le a^{1/n}\le n^{1/n}$ for $n\ge a,$ and $n^{1/n}\to 1,$ so $a^{1/n}\to 1$ by Sandwich. ✓
$n^{1/n}$ (purple) peaks at $n=3$ then decreases to $1.$ $100^{1/n}$ (blue) decreases from $100$ to $1.$ $(0.01)^{1/n}$ (orange) increases from $0.01$ to $1.$ All converge to $1.$

5. Proof of (6): $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\to 0$

Proposition: Factorials beat exponentials
For any fixed $a\in\mathbb{R},$ $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^n}{n!} = 0.$$ This says $n!$ grows much faster than $a^n$ for any fixed $a.$
✏ Strategy — Divide and Conquer It suffices to show $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\to 0$ for $a>0$ (replace $a$ by $|a|$ otherwise).

Choose $N\in\mathbb{N}$ with $N>a$ (Archimedean). For $n\ge N,$ split: $$\frac{a^n}{n!} = \underbrace{\frac{a}{1}\cdot\frac{a}{2}\cdots\frac{a}{N}}_{\text{first }N\text{ factors}=:C} \cdot\underbrace{\frac{a}{N+1}\cdot\frac{a}{N+2}\cdots\frac{a}{n}}_{\text{remaining }n-N\text{ factors}}.$$ Each factor in the second group is $\dfrac{a}{k}\le\dfrac{a}{N+1}=:r<1.$ So the second group is $\le r^{n-N}.$ Hence $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\le C\cdot r^{n-N}=C'\cdot r^n\to 0.$
Proof. We may assume $a>0$ (replacing $a$ by $|a|$ if needed). By the Archimedean Property there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ with $N>a.$ For $n\ge N,$ write: $$\frac{a^n}{n!} = \frac{a^N}{N!}\cdot\frac{a}{N+1}\cdot\frac{a}{N+2}\cdots\frac{a}{n}.$$ Set $C=\dfrac{a^N}{N!}>0$ and $r=\dfrac{a}{N+1}<1$ (since $N+1>a$). Each factor $\dfrac{a}{k}\le r$ for $k\ge N+1.$ Therefore: $$0\le\frac{a^n}{n!}\le C\cdot r^{n-N} = \frac{C}{r^N}\cdot r^n.$$ Since $r\in(0,1),$ by limit (1) we have $r^n\to 0,$ so $\dfrac{C}{r^N}r^n\to 0.$ By the Sandwich Lemma, $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

8. Cesàro Means (Theorem 2.5.3)

Theorem 2.5.3 — Cesàro Means
Let $x_n\to 0.$ Define the arithmetic means (averages) $$s_n \;:=\; \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}.$$ Then $s_n\to 0.$

More generally: if $x_n\to L,$ then $s_n\to L.$
✏ Divide and Conquer Strategy We need to estimate $|s_n|=\left|\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n x_k\right|.$ Split the sum at index $N$ (to be chosen): $$|s_n|\le\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k| + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=N+1}^n|x_k|.$$ Second part: since $x_k\to 0,$ for $k>N$ we have $|x_k|<\varepsilon/2.$ The second sum $\le\dfrac{n-N}{n}\cdot\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$
First part: the numerator $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k|$ is a fixed finite number $M.$ Choose $n$ large enough that $M/n<\varepsilon/2.$ Then the first part $<\varepsilon/2.$ Combining: $|s_n|<\varepsilon.$
Proof (case $x_n\to 0$). Let $\varepsilon>0.$ Since $x_n\to 0,$ there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $|x_k|<\varepsilon/2$ for all $k>N.$

For $n>N$: $$|s_n| = \left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n x_k\right| \le \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k| + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=N+1}^n|x_k|.$$ The second sum: each $|x_k|<\varepsilon/2,$ so $\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{k=N+1}^n|x_k|\le\dfrac{n-N}{n}\cdot\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$

Set $M=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k|$ (finite). Choose $n_0>N$ with $M/n_0<\varepsilon/2.$ For $n\ge n_0$: $\dfrac{M}{n}\le\dfrac{M}{n_0}<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$

So for all $n\ge n_0$: $|s_n|<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.$ $\quad\blacksquare$

General case $x_n\to L$: Apply the above to $y_n=x_n-L\to 0.$ Its Cesàro means are $\dfrac{s_n^{(x)}-nL}{n}=s_n^{(y)}\to 0,$ so $s_n^{(x)}\to L.$ $\blacksquare$
Remark: Converse is False
If $s_n\to L,$ it does not follow that $x_n\to L.$ Example: $x_n=(-1)^n$ diverges, but $s_n=\dfrac{0\text{ or }1/n}{1}\to 0$ (the partial sums are $0$ or $-1/n$).
Actually: $s_n = 0$ for odd $n$ and $s_n = 0$ for even $n$ here, so $s_n\to 0.$ Cesàro convergence is weaker than ordinary convergence.
Corollary
If $x_n\to L,$ then the arithmetic means $\dfrac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\to L.$

In particular: if $x_n=f(n)$ for some function converging to $L,$ its running average also converges to $L$ — useful in ergodic theory and in numerical approximation.

Q1. Using limit (1), show $\left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right)^n\to 0$ and find the smallest $N$ such that $\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^n<0.01$ for all $n>N.$

Solution. $r=3/4\in[0,1),$ so $(3/4)^n\to 0$ by limit (1). ✓
$(3/4)^n<0.01\iff n\ln(3/4)<\ln(0.01)\iff n>\dfrac{\ln 0.01}{\ln(3/4)} =\dfrac{-4.605}{-0.2877}\approx 16.00.$ So $N=16$ works: for $n\ge 17,$ $(3/4)^n<0.01.$

Q2. Use limit (3) to show $n(0.9)^n\to 0,$ and deduce that $n^2(0.9)^n\to 0.$

Solution. By limit (3) with $r=0.9$: $n(0.9)^n\to 0.$ ✓

For $n^2(0.9)^n$: write $r'=\sqrt{0.9}\approx 0.949<1.$ Then $n^2(0.9)^n=n^2(r')^{2n}=\bigl[n(r')^n\bigr]^2\cdot\dfrac{1}{n^0}$...
Better: $n^2(0.9)^n=n\cdot[n(0.9)^{n/2}]\cdot(0.9)^{n/2}/(n)$. Most directly: by limit (3) applied twice, $n\cdot r^{n/2}\to 0$ and $n^2 r^n=(n r^{n/2})^2\cdot r^0$... The cleanest approach:
$n^2(0.9)^n = n\cdot[n(0.9)^n]\to n\cdot 0= 0$? No — $n$ diverges.
Use: write $0.9=(0.95)^2$. Then $n^2(0.9)^n=n^2(0.95)^{2n}=[n(0.95)^n]^2\to 0$ since $n(0.95)^n\to 0.$ ✓

Q4. Show that $\dfrac{n^{100}}{1.01^n}\to 0.$ (This illustrates that exponentials beat any polynomial.)

Solution. Write $1.01^n=e^{n\ln 1.01}.$ This grows exponentially. By limit (6) with $a=1/\ln 1.01\approx 100.5$: $\dfrac{(1/\ln 1.01)^n}{n!}\to 0.$

More directly: by limit (3) applied $100$ times inductively, $n^{100}r^n\to 0$ for any $r\in(0,1).$ Take $r=1/1.01.$ Then $\dfrac{n^{100}}{1.01^n}=n^{100}\cdot r^n\to 0.$ ✓

Q5. Prove: $\sqrt[n]{n!}\to\infty.$ (Hint: show $\sqrt[n]{n!}\ge\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{2}$ for even $n.$)

Proof. For even $n$: $n!\ge\left(\dfrac{n}{2}\right)^{n/2}$ since at least $n/2$ of the factors in $n!$ are $\ge n/2.$ So $(n!)^{1/n}\ge\left(\dfrac{n}{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{n/2}\to\infty.$ $\blacksquare$

By Stirling's formula (not proved here): $(n!)^{1/n}\sim n/e\to\infty.$

Q1. Show that $\dfrac{\cos n}{n^2}\to 0.$

Proof. Since $-1\le\cos n\le 1$: $-\dfrac{1}{n^2}\le\dfrac{\cos n}{n^2}\le\dfrac{1}{n^2}.$ Both bounds $\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $\dfrac{\cos n}{n^2}\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q2. Prove $x_n:=\dfrac{n}{n^2+1}\to 0.$

Proof. $0\le\dfrac{n}{n^2+1}\le\dfrac{n}{n^2}=\dfrac{1}{n}\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $x_n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q3. Show $y_n=\dfrac{1}{n+1}+\dfrac{1}{n+2}+\cdots+\dfrac{1}{2n}\to\ln 2.$
(You may use the fact that this equals a Riemann sum; or just show it is trapped between known bounds.)

Hint / Bounds. $y_n$ has $n$ terms. The smallest term is $\tfrac{1}{2n}$ and the largest is $\tfrac{1}{n+1}.$ So $\dfrac{n}{2n}\le y_n\le\dfrac{n}{n+1},$ i.e., $\dfrac{1}{2}\le y_n\le\dfrac{n}{n+1}.$ The right bound $\to 1,$ but this Sandwich only gives $1/2\le\lim y_n\le 1.$

The exact limit is $\ln 2\approx 0.693$ (from the Riemann sum of $\int_1^2(1/x)\,dx$), which requires integral calculus to establish precisely. The Sandwich here confirms $y_n$ is bounded; the exact limit needs a refined argument.

Q4. Suppose $(a_n)$ is bounded and $r^n\to 0$ for some $|r|<1.$ Prove $a_n r^n\to 0.$

Proof. By Application 6: $(a_n)$ bounded and $r^n\to 0$ (which means $x_n=r^n\to 0$). By Application 6, $a_n r^n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Direct: $|a_n|\le B,$ so $-B|r|^n\le a_nr^n\le B|r|^n.$ Since $|r|^n\to 0,$ both bounds $\to 0.$ Sandwich gives $a_nr^n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q6. Show that $\left(1+\dfrac{1}{n}\right)^{n+1}$ is monotone decreasing and bounded below by $e.$ Conclude it also converges to $e.$

Solution. Let $b_n=\left(1+\tfrac{1}{n}\right)^{n+1}.$ Apply AM-GM to $n+1$ copies of $\left(1-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\right)$ and one copy of $1$, or note $b_n/b_{n+1}=\left(\dfrac{(n+1)^2}{n(n+2)}\right)^{n+1}\cdot\dfrac{n+2}{n+1}>1.$

$(b_n)$ decreases. Since $b_n=a_n\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)\ge a_n\ge 2,$ it is bounded below. By MCT, $b_n\to L.$ But $b_n=a_n\cdot\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)\to e\cdot 1=e,$ so $L=e.$ We have $a_n\le e\le b_n$ for all $n.$ ✓

Q7. Use the series $e=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!}$ to prove $e>2.7.$

Proof. $e = 1+1+\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{1}{24}+\dfrac{1}{120}+\cdots$ $> 1+1+0.5+0.1\overline{6}+0.04166\ldots+0.00833\ldots = 2.71666\ldots>2.7.$ $\blacksquare$

Q8. The proof that $e$ is irrational uses $T=q!\cdot R_q\ge 1$ and $T<1/q.$
(a) Write out $T$ explicitly for $q=3.$ (b) Verify $T\in(0,1)$ numerically.

Solution. $q=3$: $T=3!\cdot R_3=6\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{4!}+\dfrac{1}{5!}+\cdots\right) =\dfrac{6}{24}+\dfrac{6}{120}+\cdots=\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{20}+\dfrac{1}{120}+\cdots$ $=0.25+0.05+0.00833\ldots+\cdots\approx 0.3096\ldots$
$0<T\approx 0.31<1.$ ✓ Not an integer, consistent with $e$ being irrational.

Also $T\le 1/q=1/3\approx 0.333,$ check: $0.31<0.333.$ ✓

Q9. Prove that $e$ is not a root of any polynomial of degree $2$ with integer coefficients, i.e., $ae^2+be+c\ne 0$ for $a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}$ not all zero. (Note: This is a weaker statement than $e$ being transcendental — harder to prove in general. Give an argument using the irrationality of $e.$)

Partial Argument. If $ae^2+be+c=0$ with $a\ne 0,$ then $e^2 = -\tfrac{b}{a}e-\tfrac{c}{a}.$ This would mean $e^2$ is a rational linear combination of $e$ and $1.$ Since $e$ is irrational, $b/a\ne 0$ would be needed, making $e$ satisfy a quadratic over $\mathbb{Q},$ i.e., $e$ would be algebraic of degree $\le 2.$ It is a theorem (Lindemann 1882) that $e$ is transcendental — not a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients — but this requires tools beyond our course. The irrationality proof does not directly give this.

Q10. Show $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^n = e^x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}.$ (Use $\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^n = \left[\left(1+\frac{1}{n/x}\right)^{n/x}\right]^x$ and limit (1+1/n)^n → e.)

Sketch. For $x>0$: $\left(1+\dfrac{x}{n}\right)^n=\left[\left(1+\dfrac{1}{n/x}\right)^{n/x}\right]^x.$ As $n\to\infty,$ $m:=n/x\to\infty$ and $\left(1+\tfrac{1}{m}\right)^m\to e.$ Hence $\left(1+\tfrac{x}{n}\right)^n=\left[\left(1+\tfrac{1}{m}\right)^m\right]^x\to e^x.$ (Continuity of $t\mapsto t^x$ is used.)

For $x<0$: use $\left(1+\tfrac{x}{n}\right)^n=1/\left(1-\tfrac{x}{n}\right)^n\cdot(\text{correction})\to e^x.$ The argument is more delicate but the result holds.

Q11. Let $a_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\dfrac{1}{k}$ (the harmonic series partial sums). Show $a_n\to\infty,$ i.e., the harmonic series diverges. (Group: $a_{2^n}>1+n/2.$)

Proof. Group: $a_{2^n}=1+\tfrac{1}{2}+\left(\tfrac13+\tfrac14\right)+\left(\tfrac15+\cdots+\tfrac18\right)+\cdots$ Each group $\left(\tfrac{1}{2^{k-1}+1}+\cdots+\tfrac{1}{2^k}\right)>2^{k-1}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2^k}=\tfrac12.$ So $a_{2^n}>1+n\cdot\tfrac12=1+n/2\to\infty.$ $\blacksquare$

Q12. Prove: if $x_n\to L,$ then the Cesàro means $s_n:=\dfrac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\to L.$ (Do this directly from the Divide and Conquer proof without reducing to $L=0.$)

Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0.$ Since $x_n\to L,$ there exists $N$ with $|x_k-L|<\varepsilon/2$ for all $k>N.$
For $n>N$: $\left|s_n-L\right|=\left|\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^n(x_k-L)}{n}\right| \le\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k-L|+\dfrac{n-N}{n}\cdot\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$
Set $M=\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k-L|.$ Choose $n_0$ with $M/n_0<\varepsilon/2.$ For $n\ge n_0$: $|s_n-L|<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.$ $\blacksquare$

Q13 (Exercise 2.5.2 from text). Let $a>1.$ Show $\dfrac{n}{a^n}\to 0.$ (Use $nr^n\to 0$ with $r=1/a<1.$)

Proof. $\dfrac{n}{a^n}=n\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)^n=n\cdot r^n$ where $r=1/a\in(0,1).$ By limit (3), $nr^n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q14. Show that $\left(\dfrac{n^2+3n}{n^2+1}\right)^n\to e^3.$

Solution. Write $\dfrac{n^2+3n}{n^2+1}=1+\dfrac{3n-1}{n^2+1}=1+\dfrac{3-1/n}{n+1/n} \approx 1+\dfrac{3}{n}$ for large $n.$ More precisely: $$\left(\frac{n^2+3n}{n^2+1}\right)^n=\left(1+\frac{3n-1}{n^2+1}\right)^n.$$ Let $u_n=\dfrac{3n-1}{n^2+1}.$ Then $nu_n=\dfrac{n(3n-1)}{n^2+1}\to 3.$ Since $\left(1+\dfrac{x}{n}\right)^n\to e^x$ and $nu_n\to 3,$ the expression $\to e^3.$ $\blacksquare$

Q15. Prove: $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdots(2n)}=0.$

Proof. Let $P_n=\dfrac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot4\cdots(2n)}.$ Note that $P_n^2\le\dfrac{1}{2n+1}$ by the Wallis product bound (each factor $\dfrac{2k-1}{2k}\cdot\dfrac{2k-1}{2k}<\dfrac{2k-1}{2k}\cdot\dfrac{2k+1}{2k+1}$, telescoping). More directly: $P_n\le\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\to 0$ (Wallis), so $P_n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q16 (Bonus). Prove that $e$ is not equal to $8/3,$ $5/2,$ or any simple fraction by using the numerical bounds $2<e<3$ and the alternating series for $e-S_n.$

Illustration. $S_5=1+1+\tfrac12+\tfrac16+\tfrac{1}{24}+\tfrac{1}{120}=\tfrac{109}{45}\approx 2.7\overline{1}.$ $S_6=S_5+\tfrac{1}{720}\approx 2.71805.$ $S_5<e<S_5+\tfrac{1}{6!}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1-1/7}$... one can trap $e$ in the interval $(2.71828,2.71829)$ using $S_8.$ Since $8/3\approx 2.6\overline{6},$ $5/2=2.5$ — neither lies in this interval. More generally, the irrationality proof handles all fractions at once. $\blacksquare$

7. Self-Assessment — Part B: Proofs & Extensions

Q6. Prove: if $0\le a_n\le\dfrac{1}{n}$ for all $n,$ then $a_n\to 0.$ Is the condition $a_n\ge 0$ necessary?

Answer. $0\le a_n\le 1/n.$ Both bounds $\to 0.$ By Sandwich, $a_n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$

The condition $a_n\ge 0$ is needed to apply Sandwich (lower bound $=0$). Without it: $a_n$ could be e.g. $a_n=-n,$ satisfying $a_n\le 1/n$ but $a_n\not\to 0.$ The Sandwich still works with the bounds $-1/n\le a_n\le 1/n$ if that holds; the key is that both bounds must be given.

Q7. Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sin n+\cos n}{n}$ and justify fully.

Solution. $|\sin n+\cos n|\le|\sin n|+|\cos n|\le 1+1=2.$ So $-\dfrac{2}{n}\le\dfrac{\sin n+\cos n}{n}\le\dfrac{2}{n}.$ Both bounds $\to 0.$ Limit $= 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q8. Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous at $a.$ Let $r_n\to a$ with $r_n\in\mathbb{Q}.$ Prove $f(r_n)\to f(a)$ using sequential continuity. Conclude that $f$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is determined by its values on $\mathbb{Q}.$

Proof. By sequential continuity: any sequence $x_n\to a$ gives $f(x_n)\to f(a).$ Apply this with $x_n=r_n\in\mathbb{Q}$: $f(r_n)\to f(a).$ ✓

Now suppose $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is also continuous with $g(r)=f(r)$ for all $r\in\mathbb{Q}.$ For any $a\in\mathbb{R},$ choose rationals $r_n\to a$ (Application 4). Then $f(a)=\lim f(r_n)=\lim g(r_n)=g(a).$ So $f=g.$ $\blacksquare$

Q9. Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+k}}\to 1.$ (Use the same technique as Application 8.)

Proof. Each term satisfies $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}}\le\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+k}}\le\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}$ for $k=1,\ldots,n.$ Summing $n$ terms: $\dfrac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}}\le\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+k}}\le\dfrac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}.$
Both bounds $\to 1$ (divide top and bottom by $n$). Sandwich gives limit $=1.$ $\blacksquare$

Q10. Prove: for any $k\in\mathbb{N},$ $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^k}{n!}=0.$ (Hint: bound from above by a geometric sequence for large $n.$)

Proof. For $n>2k$: $\dfrac{n^k}{n!}=\underbrace{\dfrac{n}{n}\cdot\dfrac{n}{n-1}\cdots\dfrac{n}{n-k+1}}_{\le\,2^k}\cdot\dfrac{1}{(n-k)!}.$
More directly: choose $N>2k.$ For $n\ge N$: $\dfrac{n^k}{n!}=\dfrac{n\cdot n\cdots n}{1\cdot2\cdots n} \le\dfrac{n^k}{(n/2)^{n/2}}\to 0$ (exponentials beat polynomials).

Sandwich: $0\le\dfrac{n^k}{n!}\le C\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n\to 0$ for suitable $C.$ $\blacksquare$

Q11. The Nested Interval Theorem requires $b_n-a_n\to 0.$ Give an example of nested closed intervals $[a_n,b_n]$ with $\bigcap_n[a_n,b_n]$ being an entire interval (not a single point).

Example. Take $[a_n,b_n]=[0,1+1/n].$ Then $[a_1,b_1]=[0,2]\supseteq[0,3/2]\supseteq\cdots$ and $\bigcap_n[0,1+1/n]=[0,1]$ — an interval, not a point.

The lengths $b_n-a_n=1+1/n\to 1\ne 0,$ so the hypothesis $b_n-a_n\to 0$ fails, and accordingly uniqueness fails. ✓

8. Self-Assessment — Part C: Challenge

Q12. Prove: if $a_n\ge 0$ for all $n$ and $a_n\to L,$ then $L\ge 0.$ (Use Sandwich with lower bound $0.$)

Proof. We have $0\le a_n$ for all $n.$ Taking limits: $0\le\lim a_n=L.$

More carefully: suppose $L<0.$ Set $\varepsilon=-L/2>0.$ There exists $N$ with $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon=-L/2$ for $n\ge N,$ i.e., $a_n<L-L/2=L/2<0.$ But $a_n\ge 0$ — contradiction. So $L\ge 0.$ $\blacksquare$

Q13. Let $\alpha=\text{lub}(A)$ with $\alpha\notin A.$ Using Application 5, show that $\alpha$ is a limit point of $A$ (i.e., every open interval around $\alpha$ contains a point of $A$ other than $\alpha$).

Proof. By Application 5, there exists $(a_n)$ with $a_n\in A$ and $a_n\to\alpha.$ Since $\alpha\notin A,$ we have $a_n\ne\alpha$ for all $n.$ For any $\varepsilon>0,$ eventually $|a_n-\alpha|<\varepsilon,$ so $a_n\in(\alpha-\varepsilon,\alpha+\varepsilon)\cap A$ with $a_n\ne\alpha.$ Hence $\alpha$ is a limit point of $A.$ $\blacksquare$

Q14 (Bolzano–Weierstrass via Nested Intervals). Let $(x_n)$ be a bounded sequence: $|x_n|\le M.$ Use the Nested Interval Theorem (via bisection) to prove $(x_n)$ has a convergent subsequence.

Proof sketch. Start with $J_0=[-M,M].$ Bisect into $[-M,0]$ and $[0,M].$ At least one half contains infinitely many terms; call it $J_1.$ Repeat: bisect $J_1$ and choose the half with infinitely many terms — call it $J_2.$ Continue: we get nested closed intervals $J_0\supseteq J_1\supseteq J_2\supseteq\cdots$ each of length $M\cdot 2^{1-k}\to 0,$ each containing infinitely many terms of $(x_n).$

By the Nested Interval Theorem, $\bigcap_k J_k=\{c\}$ for some $c.$ Pick indices $n_1<n_2<\cdots$ with $x_{n_k}\in J_k.$ Then $x_{n_k}\in J_k$ and $|x_{n_k}-c|\le\text{length}(J_k)\to 0.$ So $x_{n_k}\to c.$ $\blacksquare$

Q15 (Cesàro Means). Suppose $x_n\to 0.$ Define the arithmetic mean $s_n=\dfrac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}.$ Use the Sandwich Theorem (Divide and Conquer) to show $s_n\to 0.$

Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0.$ Choose $N$ with $|x_k|<\varepsilon/2$ for $k>N.$
For $n>N$: $$|s_n|\le\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=N+1}^n|x_k| \le\frac{M}{n}+\frac{n-N}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$$ where $M=\sum_{k=1}^N|x_k|$ is fixed. Choose $n_0>N$ with $M/n_0<\varepsilon/2.$ For $n\ge n_0$: $|s_n|<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.$

Since $-|s_n|\le s_n\le|s_n|$ and $|s_n|\to 0$: Sandwich gives $s_n\to 0.$ $\blacksquare$
